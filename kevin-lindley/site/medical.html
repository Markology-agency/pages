The Business Side Nobody Teaches in Training

The calls I get from physicians sound a lot alike. The first locums check arrives with nothing withheld, and there's no plan for what to set aside. The accountant says the home office is a red flag. Or the accountant stops answering emails halfway through the year, and the books haven't been touched since January.

Some people inherited a parent's CPA and never felt comfortable asking questions. Others are doing it themselves with software and a chatbot, and they aren't sure any of it is right.

None of this is a knock on physicians. You spent a decade getting very good at one thing. The switch to self-employed life is hard, and it takes a few years before it feels normal.

Most of the physicians I work with are in one of three situations.

what we handle

What We Handle for Physicians

The W-2 versus 1099 decision

A W-2 salary and the same number on a 1099 aren't equal. As an employee, your employer pays half of your Social Security and Medicare, and usually health insurance and a 401(k) match. On a 1099 you pay self-employment tax on all of it yourself. Before you sign a contract, I'll show you what the 1099 offer has to be to come out even.

The S-corp decision, with the math

I don't recommend an S-corp by default. If you already have a large W-2, it's paying a lot of your payroll tax already, and the S-corp becomes a lot less effective. If you'll be full-time 1099 for several years, it usually pays. Either way, we run a projection before anyone files Form 2553.

A reasonable salary you can defend

The IRS expects an S-corp owner to take reasonable compensation. The easy way to think about it is this. If you left for a month and hired someone to cover, you wouldn't pay them all of your revenue. We set the salary with your specialty, your retirement goals, and the payroll tax all in view.

Locums and more than one state

The technically correct answer is that you file wherever you worked. After that, it comes down to how much you earned there and how that state treats it. We sort out where you have to file and how a mid-year move changes the plan.

A practice started in the right order

Entity, business bank account, payroll, and a retirement plan that works once you have staff. Fixing a structure later is a lot harder than setting it up right the first time. If attorneys are drafting the formation documents, I'm happy to get on a call with them.

across state lines

How Do Locums and Telehealth Work Across State Lines?

Locums takes you wherever the shifts are, and telehealth can put patients from several states on your schedule in one afternoon. Each of those can come with its own state filing, and a lot of physicians only find out when a notice shows up.

The technically correct answer is that you file in the states where you worked. After that it comes down to how much you earned there, how that state treats nonresidents, and, if your home state has an income tax, how it credits what you paid somewhere else. Texas has no state personal income tax, so for a Texas resident the out-of-state work is usually where any state tax comes from.

Telehealth adds a wrinkle, because your licenses, where you physically sit, and where your patients are can all point to different states. We look at each of them, then tell you which returns you need and what to set aside for each one.

Your entity can live in a different state from you, too. Plenty of the physicians I work with live in Texas and formed their entity somewhere else, which is fine as long as the registrations and filings in each state are kept up. Leave some time for it. One telemedicine client of ours started forming a Florida entity in February, and the state didn't finish it until the middle of March.

Once the states are sorted out, the next big decision is usually retirement.

retirement

Which Retirement Plan Fits a Physician With 1099 Income?

For physicians with self-employed income, the retirement plan is often one of the biggest deductions available. The right plan depends on how much of your income is 1099, whether you already have a plan at work, and whether you have staff.

Solo 401(k) or SEP IRA

Both let you put a lot away from 1099 income, and both are simple to open at a brokerage. If you already contribute to a 401(k) at your W-2 job, your employee deferral limit is per person across every plan, so the two have to be coordinated. A SEP IRA balance is counted when you convert to a Roth, which can make a backdoor Roth IRA messy. That's why I usually lean toward a solo 401(k) for physicians who want to do both.

Defined benefit and cash balance plans

When the 1099 income is large and steady, a defined benefit plan, including the cash balance version, can let you contribute and deduct much more each year than a 401(k). It comes with an actuary, an annual administration cost, and a funding commitment, so it only fits at higher income levels. For the physicians it does fit, very few other strategies come close.

When there's an S-corp

Your salary sets how much you can put into the solo 401(k), so the salary decision and the retirement decision get made together. A higher salary costs more payroll tax and opens up more room in the plan. We find the point that matches what you want to save.

Once you have staff

Employees change which plans make sense, and a plan that covers staff has to be set up earlier in the year so they have time to enroll. Some states also have their own retirement program rules for employers, and we check those too.

I also like clients to have savings in more than one tax bucket: money that's tax-deferred, money in a Roth, and money in a regular taxable account you can reach without penalties. That gives you choices later, when tax rates may look very different. We don't give investment advice, so we work with your financial advisor on the investing and handle the tax side ourselves.

If you're thinking about opening your own practice, the order you set it up in matters even more.

starting a practice

Opening a Medical Practice in the Right Order

If you've done 1099 work, you've already run a very small version of a practice. The mechanics are mostly the same. Owning a practice adds staff payroll, supplies, and a lot more paperwork, and all of it is much easier to set up right the first time than to untangle later.

1. The structure, with your attorneys

Your healthcare attorneys draft the formation documents. With more than one physician, that can mean separate professional entities plus a management company that provides services to them. I ask the attorneys for a simple chart showing who owns what and how the management fee works, and then I make sure the tax structure matches what they drafted. I'm happy to get on a call with them, with you on the line too.

2. A bank account and books from day one

A dedicated business account, and a bookkeeping file set up before the first patient. Startup costs usually go on personal cards before the business account exists, so keep a list and we'll record them properly.

3. Owner pay and staff payroll

Your own salary is a tax lever, so I set it and adjust it as the year goes. Staff payroll runs through your payroll provider, and we make sure the state registrations and filings are in place before the first paycheck.

4. A retirement plan that works with employees

Chosen early enough in the year that staff can enroll, and designed so it still does what you need it to do for your own savings.

situations we check for

Situations We Check For With Physicians

These don’t apply to everyone, so they shouldn’t crowd the main plan. When one of them fits your situation, it can change the numbers a lot.

Working in more than one state−

Locums shifts, telehealth patients, a move partway through the year, or an entity formed outside your home state. We figure out which returns you need and build the payments into your road map.

Health insurance and an HSA+

How your coverage is paid changes how it's deducted. If you're self-employed and buy your own coverage, the premiums can be deductible on your personal return, as long as you weren't eligible for a subsidized plan through your own employer or your spouse's that month. With an S-corp, the company pays or reimburses the premiums and adds them to your W-2, and then you can usually deduct them on your personal return. An HSA needs qualifying coverage, which is usually a high-deductible health plan. Some solo physicians look at sharing an entity with another physician to get group coverage, and that needs careful structuring.

Rental property and your own office building+

A short-term rental you run yourself, a long-term rental, or a building your practice rents from you each have their own rules. When you rent your office to your own practice, the building and the practice can sometimes be treated as one activity, which changes how much a cost segregation study helps. We explain what comes back when you sell before you commit.

Hiring and growth+

A first medical assistant, a second location, or a new partner. Each one touches payroll, state registrations, the books, the retirement plan, and your own pay, so we look at it before the decision is final.

Questions Physicians Ask Us

What should a physician look for in a CPA?
−

Someone who plans the year with you instead of just filing it, and who has worked with physician income before. Ask who you'll actually talk to, how fast they answer, and whether questions during the year cost extra.

What changes if I go from W-2 to 1099?
−

Nobody withholds tax for you anymore, so you make estimated payments through the year. You also pay both halves of Social Security and Medicare. In exchange, you can deduct the expenses of earning that income and pick your own retirement plan. I usually suggest an LLC and a dedicated business bank account from day one.

Should I elect S-corp status on my locums income?
−

It depends on how much of your income is 1099 and how long it will stay that way. If you'll be full-time 1099 for several years, it's usually worth running the numbers. If you already have a large W-2, or you're heading back to a W-2 job soon, it often costs more than it saves.

Is the home office deduction a red flag?
−

No. If you have a room you use regularly and exclusively for the business, like a spare bedroom, it's a normal deduction. If it qualifies as your principal place of business, the drive from home to a hospital or clinic can become a business mile too.
