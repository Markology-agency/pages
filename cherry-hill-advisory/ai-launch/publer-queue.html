AI is living in nearly everything the organization is doing. Whether you know it or not.

Your third parties are probably using it, even if you have decided not to. The HR platform added an AI summarizer. The contract review tool quietly turned on auto-extraction. The fraud detection vendor changed its scoring model last quarter and the release notes mentioned "enhanced detection." That is AI.

Which brings up a question I get almost every webinar:

If you tell a third-party AI vendor your data cannot be used to train their model, how do you actually know they're complying?

The honest answer is: it comes down to your right-to-audit provisions, your assessment of their control environment, and a level of earned trust over time. Absolute assurance is not achievable. Reasonable assurance is the goal, and the controls have to support it.

Which means the inventory and identification step in your AI audit work is non-negotiable. You cannot assess what you have not first found. And the third-party surface is where the unknown AI hides.

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act was signed 23 years ago today, on July 30, 2002, after Enron and WorldCom proved that audited financial statements were only as trustworthy as the controls behind them.

The mechanism Congress chose, independent assessment of internal controls over financial reporting, is the same mechanism every Big-4 attestation and every internal audit function has refined since. SOX did not invent the discipline. It made it a public-company requirement and pushed it up the org chart.

The reason SOX still matters is not the 2002 framework. It is the through line. The financial systems being audited today are increasingly AI-enabled. The journal entries, the reconciliations, the close, the disclosures. If a model is involved in any of that, the SOX question is no longer "did we test the application." The SOX question is "do we know what the model did, and can we prove it."

Trust is the work. SOX was just the moment we decided to require it.
