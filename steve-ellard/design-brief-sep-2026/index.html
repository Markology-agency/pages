The client in one paragraph

Steven M. Ellard CPA is a local CPA firm on Cape Cod, Massachusetts. Steve is the neighbourhood accountant, not a national brand, and the whole positioning rests on being local, reachable and proactive. The clients are year-round Cape business owners and seasonal operators: contractors, restaurants, retail, plus Cape individuals with a rental or seasonal income wrinkle. The tone is warm and dry-witted, never corporate and never salesy.

The single most important visual instruction: this has to look like it came from a firm on the Cape, not from a stock-photo template. If the creative could be swapped onto any accounting firm in any state without anyone noticing, it hasn't done its job.

Rules that apply to all nine

  • Text on the image stays short. The primary text does the talking; the image is not where the

argument gets made. Aim for six words or fewer on the frame.

  • Keep overlay text off the bottom-right corner, where Meta stacks the CTA button on some

placements.

  • No stock photography of handshakes, skylines, suited people at conference tables, or anyone

pointing at a laptop. It reads as generic and it kills the local claim.

  • No fake data. No invented dashboards, chart mockups with made-up numbers, or screenshots of

software the firm doesn't use.

  • No badges, seals or ribbon graphics implying an award or certification that hasn't been earned.
  • Nothing implying a guaranteed outcome, a refund amount, or a savings figure. This matters most

on concept 2, where the whole idea sits near a savings claim. The copy has been written carefully to stay on the right side of that line, so the design must not put a number back in. No dollar signs, no percentages, no up-and-to-the-right arrows.

  • Accessible contrast. Slate on off-white is fine; red on slate is not.

How these have to work

Every frame is a big-type statement card. The line is the graphic. No mood photography, no abstract treatments, no illustrations of concepts. Someone thumbing past at speed reads one sentence, recognises themselves in it, and stops.

The rule for every line below: it names a specific thing the reader has actually done or is actually worried about. Not a category, not a benefit, not a tagline.

Type is huge. On a 1080x1350 the statement should be roughly 90 to 130px, filling most of the frame, tight leading, left aligned. Brand mark small at the bottom. Nothing else competing.

Concept 1: Payroll and seasonal crews

Brand: Atlantic Payroll

On-frame text

You paid your summer crew as contractors.

Massachusetts probably disagrees.

Why it stops the scroll: a large number of Cape businesses did exactly this in June, quickly, because the season was starting. It names a specific decision the reader made a few months ago and puts a real authority behind the doubt. That's a click.

Massachusetts uses a strict three-prong test for whether someone is an employee or a contractor, and it's genuinely harder to pass than most owners assume, so the line is accurate. Keep the hedge word ("probably") in. Do not upgrade it to "is wrong" or "you're breaking the law".

Layout: two-line statement, first line slate #3D4557, second line deep red #9D002B and heavier weight so the turn lands. Warm off-white #E9E7DB ground. Atlantic Payroll wordmark bottom-left, small.

Alternate for the second ratio, same idea in list form:

Three payroll mistakes Cape businesses make every summer

1. Paying the seasonal crew as contractors

2. Final paychecks on the wrong schedule

3. Records that don't show who worked when

Numbered-list cards perform. Set the numerals large in red, the items in slate.

Concept 2: The April tax bill

Brand: Steve Ellard CPA

On-frame text

If your accountant only calls you in March,

that's the problem.

Why it stops the scroll: it's an accusation the reader immediately tests against their own accountant, and for a lot of them the answer is yes. It converts your point about year-round planning into something the reader can check in two seconds, which vague planning language never does.

Second option, equally direct:

Nothing your accountant does in April

changes your April tax bill.

Layout: same big-type treatment. The turn line in deep red, heavier. Steve Ellard CPA logo bottom-left.

Hard compliance line: no dollar figures, no percentages, no charts, no upward arrows, no "save" or "savings" anywhere on the frame. This concept sits next to a savings claim and a single number on the image turns it into one. Words only.

Concept 3: Off-season cleanup

Brand: Steve Ellard CPA

On-frame text

Your books haven't been touched

since Memorial Day.

Why it stops the scroll: it's a specific accusation with a specific date, and for a seasonal Cape business it's usually true. Far stronger than talking about planning season, because the reader doesn't have to translate anything.

Alternate:

September is when Cape businesses fix their books.

October is when they wish they had.

Layout: same statement-card system. If a real off-season Cape photo is available it can sit behind the type at low contrast, but only if the type stays fully legible. Type beats photo every time here. A generic coastal stock shot is worse than no photo.

Consistency across the set

All nine frames use the same system: off-white ground, slate statement, deep red on the turn line, small brand mark bottom-left. Someone who sees two of these should recognise the third. The only thing that changes between concepts is the sentence and which logo sits at the bottom.

Handback

Drop the nine files plus a single contact-sheet preview into the client folder. If a line is breaking badly at one ratio, change the line break, never the wording.
