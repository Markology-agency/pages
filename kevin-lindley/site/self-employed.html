The First Check With Nothing Taken Out

For most people the moment it gets real is the first payment. You look at it and realize nobody took any taxes out. Then the questions start. How much do I set aside? Do I need an LLC? Can I deduct my car? Do I have to save every receipt?

A W-2 is easy to take for granted. You type a few numbers into tax software and you're done. Once you're self-employed, the taxes, the retirement plan, the health insurance, and the paperwork are all yours now.

It gets to be second nature after a few years. The first year is where a CPA earns the fee.

how it works

The First Year, Set Up in Order

1. Entity and account

An LLC and a dedicated business bank account. A single-member LLC is taxed just like you earned the money personally, so there's no tax change. What it gives you is a clean line between business and personal. On the Form W-9 you give clients, you use the LLC's EIN instead of your Social Security number.

2. What to set aside

A simple percentage of every deposit goes into a separate savings account. The quarterly estimated payments come out of that account. In December we build a projection and set the last payment to the real number.

3. Deductions with a reason behind them

If you wouldn't spend the money except for the business, it's likely deductible. That includes a home office, your phone and laptop for the business share, licensing, continuing education, and business mileage.

4. S-corp and retirement, when they pay

Not before. The S-corp has to save more than the payroll and extra return cost. The retirement plan, whether a solo 401(k) or a SEP, gets picked once we can see what the year looks like.

You Don't Need a Shoebox of Receipts

This is one of the first things people ask. If every business expense runs through the business account, the bank statement shows the vendor and the amount, and that covers most of it.

Receipts matter for bigger purchases that could go either way. An online order could be office furniture or it could be a TV. Keep those.

The vehicle is the one place I'm careful. You'll need a mileage log, and I almost never put a car at 100% business use unless it's a branded company car. The IRS gives you two ways to deduct it, the standard mileage rate or actual expenses. For an inexpensive car you drive a lot, mileage usually wins. For an expensive car you drive less, actual expenses can make more sense, as long as you plan for what happens when you trade it in.

Questions the Self-Employed Ask

Do I need an LLC as an independent contractor?
−

Not to be taxed as a business. Your income goes on Schedule C either way. I still recommend one with its own bank account. It separates the business from your personal finances and makes a later S-corp election cleaner.

How much should I set aside for taxes?
−

It depends on your deductions, your retirement contributions, your spouse's income, and your state. I won't give one number for everybody. You'll get your number in the road map, and we set it a little high early in the year so December isn't a scramble.

Do I need to keep receipts for everything?
−

Not for every small purchase. You do need to be able to support your expenses if you're ever audited. When every expense runs through the business account, the statement handles most of that. Keep receipts for larger purchases that aren't obviously for the business.

When is an S-corp worth it?
−

When your self-employment income is steady and large enough that the self-employment tax savings clearly beat the cost of payroll and a second return. If you also have a big W-2, the benefit shrinks. We run the math before anyone files Form 2553.
