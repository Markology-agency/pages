The one idea to take away

Match what you ask for to the mood the person is in

The same person behaves differently depending on what they were doing when your ad appeared. An ad works when what it asks for fits that moment. There are three moments that matter for you, and they line up with your three goals.

Was it bots?

You wondered whether bots were clicking, because Clarity showed nobody scrolling. We checked, and it looks like real people. Of 880 visits, 877 were in the US and 424 were in Indianapolis. They came on 129 different screen sizes, between 5am and 11pm. Bots don't usually look that local or that varied.

What went wrong

The ad was aimed at dentists, and the page does open on a phone. The trouble is that the ad was also allowed to reach a lot of people who aren't dentists, and the page is slow and awkward on a phone for the dentists who did tap. These are the five problems, with the biggest first.

1. The ad went to strangers

The people who saw this ad had never heard of TMA. The account has a list called "YT | Dental Video Viewers", made of people who watched your dental videos, and the checklist campaign didn't use it. Google also reports that list's size as 0, which means it is empty or still too small to use. A stranger has little reason to hand over an email for a checklist from a firm they don't know.

The fix: offer the checklist first to people who already know you: your video viewers, your website visitors and your email list.

2. The audience let in non-dentists

The campaign includes a "Dental Practice Owners" list, which is right. The same audience also includes people shopping for payroll software, tax preparation and tax services. Google reads that as "any of these", so the ad could go to someone with no link to dentistry. We can't see how many viewers were dentists, only that the audience wasn't limited to them.

The fix: keep the dental list and take the other groups out of this campaign.

3. The ad ran inside game apps

998 of the taps came from YouTube. Another 154 came from other apps where Google places ads, including puzzle games and a VPN app. A dentist looking for year-end help isn't found in a puzzle game, and taps there are often a slip of the thumb.

The fix: switch off Google's partner apps, so the ad runs on YouTube only.

4. The page is slow and long on a phone

95% of the taps were on phones. In our test the page took 8.9 seconds to show its main content on a mobile connection. The form sits about one screen down and asks for first name, last name, company and email. iPhone visitors who came from the YouTube app stayed about 6 seconds, so most left before the page finished loading.

The fix: make the page load faster, move the form to the top and ask for less.

5. Google had nothing to learn from

The campaign is told to find people who download the checklist. Google learns who to look for from the first few downloads. There haven't been any, so it kept buying the cheapest taps it could find.

The fix: use a form that opens inside the app, so the first downloads come more easily. Step 2 of the plan covers this.

Why the first problem matters most

People who already know a channel respond to its ads far more often than strangers do. A good rule of thumb is that ads shown to a warm audience convert 2 to 5 times better than ads shown to a cold one.

How to check this yourself next time

In Google Ads, open the campaign, then Insights and reports, then "When and where ads showed". That list shows the apps and channels your ad appeared on. If you see game apps there, the ad is running on Google's partner network, and you can switch that off in the ad group's channel settings.

The step that's missing between the checklist and a client

Today the path you're counting on is short. Someone subscribes on YouTube, downloads the checklist, and then hires you. That last jump is too big for most people. A checklist takes ten seconds to ask for and costs nothing. Choosing an accountant means handing over the books, payroll and taxes for years. Before they do that, an owner wants to hear from your people and see how you'd look at their business.

So the path needs a step in the middle, where a checklist reader gets a small taste of working with you. Here are three ways to do it.

A live webinar

One of your CPAs teaches a single topic for 30 minutes, such as year-end for dental practice owners, and takes questions at the end. Everyone who downloaded the checklist gets an invitation. The recording becomes a YouTube video afterward.

An open Q&A on Zoom

You hold a regular "ask an accountant" hour, and owners bring their own questions. It needs very little preparation, and people hear how your team thinks. It suits owners who aren't ready for a sales call.

A free books review

An owner gives you a look at their books, and you send back a short list of what you'd fix first. This is the closest step to becoming a client. It takes staff time, so offer it to the practices and restaurants you most want.

Where we'd start

We'd start with one live Q&A for dental practice owners in early December, because it needs the least preparation and the year-end topic is already working for you. Invite everyone who downloads the checklist, on the thank-you page and in a follow-up email. Your Financial Operations Scorecard can do a similar job for people who'd sooner not join a call, and it gets about 7 views a month today.

The ads themselves

How the ads are set up

Each campaign holds one video ad about one topic. It ran for about a month on a fixed budget of $250 or $500 and then stopped.

What the ads do well

The headlines name the topic, so a viewer knows what the video is about before tapping. "W-2 Employee or 1099 Contractor?" and "Dentrix vs Eaglesoft vs Open Dental" are good examples. The checklist ad says it is free, says who it is for, and its button says Download.

Our suggestion for the YouTube ads: one question, with a download that matches it

The audience and the platform matter, and the message matters just as much. Your checklist video opens by saying the last two months of the year are full for a dentist. That's true, and it's the reason a general message doesn't land. A busy person stops for the one problem they have today.

The 45-second ad asks three questions in a row: where does your practice stand on taxes, should you move income or expenses between tax years, and what small stuff will you regret in February. It answers none of them. Then it offers a checklist of 15 more things to do.

1. Pick one question

Take one thing the video touches on and make the whole video about it. For example: "Should you move income or expenses between 2026 and 2027?"

2. Answer it

Give the real answer in the video, with when it helps and when it doesn't. The viewer should leave knowing more than when they arrived, even if they never download anything.

3. Offer a download that matches it

The download becomes the next step for that same question. A short calculator asks a few things about their practice and tells them which way to lean, and what to ask their accountant.

Where the checklist fits

The checklist is the offer to try on Facebook and Instagram, which is step 2 of the plan. On YouTube, change the ads so each one answers one question and offers a download that matches that question.
