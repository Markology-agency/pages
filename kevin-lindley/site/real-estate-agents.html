Commission Income, Rental Losses, and a Lot of Advice From Social Media

Every ad in your feed promises a big write-off. Cost segregation, the short-term rental loophole, the heavy SUV. People tell me they take it with a grain of salt, because everyone online is trying to sell them something.

They're right to. Some of those strategies are real. Some of them look great in year one and come back when you sell.

Meanwhile the commission checks are uneven, nobody is withholding, and the quarterly estimate is a guess. Here's how we sort it out.

What We Handle for Real Estate Agents

Commission planning

Quarterly estimated payments that follow your closings, the entity question, and the S-corp math once your income is steady enough to support it.

The rental side, told straight

Long-term rentals are generally passive, and at higher incomes, losses beyond your rental income are usually suspended until you have passive income or sell. Short-term rentals can be treated differently if the stays are short and you're the one doing the work. Depreciation you take now can come back as taxable gain when you sell. We explain all of it before you buy.

Vehicle and home office, done right

Your car is deductible for the business use you can document with a mileage log. I almost never put a car at 100% business use. A home office you use regularly and exclusively for the business is a normal deduction, not a red flag.

Short-Term Rentals, Cost Segregation, and What Comes Back

A short-term rental can offset other income when two things are true. The average guest stay is 7 days or less, and you materially participate. In practice that means you're finding guests, handling bookings, and coordinating the cleaners and vendors yourself, and you're logging the hours.

A cost segregation study can front-load depreciation into the first year. That's a real benefit. What gets left out of the ads is that the deduction doesn't repeat, and much of it can come back as depreciation recapture when you sell. If you plan to sell in a couple of years, it may not be worth it.

A real estate license on its own doesn't make you a real estate professional for tax purposes. There's an hours test, and the hours have to be documented. Some full-time agents qualify. For a lot of people, a short-term rental is the easier path.

My general advice is to buy the property because it's a good investment. The tax benefit should be a bonus, not the reason.

Questions Realtors Ask

Should my commission income run through an S-corp?
−

Sometimes. Once your commissions are steady and large enough, an S-corp can lower self-employment tax. It also adds payroll, a separate return, and a reasonable salary you have to pay yourself. We run the math before anyone files Form 2553.

Can I deduct my vehicle as a real estate agent?
−

Yes, for the business use you can document. You can use the standard mileage rate or actual expenses. Mileage is usually better for a car you drive a lot. Actual expenses can be better for an expensive car, but plan for the trade-in, because the depreciation you took can come back as a gain.

Are my rental losses deductible against commission income?
−

At higher incomes, usually not for a long-term rental. Those losses are passive and generally wait until you have passive income or sell the property. A short-term rental you actively run, or qualifying as a real estate professional, can change that. We'll look at which applies to you.

Is a cost segregation study worth it?
−

It depends on how long you'll hold the property. It speeds up depreciation, which helps now, but much of it can come back when you sell. If you're holding long term, it can make sense. If you're flipping in a couple of years, often not.
