Selling a dental practice: what it's worth, how the sale works and what you keep

Maybe you're selling in six months, maybe in six years. Either way, the number that matters is what you walk away with after the loan is paid off and the taxes are paid. We help you know that number before a broker or a buyer puts one on the table.

When should you start planning the sale?

Earlier than you think. The decisions that move your sale price the most, like clean books, a market-rate owner salary and a lease with years left on it, take time to show up in your numbers.

If you're two to ten years out, you still have time to change what the practice is worth. If you're less than two years out, the work shifts to getting the numbers in order, finding the right buyer and planning the tax side of the deal.

We split our preparing-to-sell work along those same two timelines, because the to-do list is different for each.

How selling a dental practice works, step by step

Every sale is different, but most follow the same path. Here's how it usually goes, and where we come in.

1. Decide what you want. Do you want to walk away completely, keep working a few days a week, or sell to someone already in the building? Your answer changes who the right buyer is and how the deal should be set up.

2. Know your numbers. We value the practice the way a careful buyer and their bank will, and we show you what you'd walk away with after debt and taxes.

3. Clean up what a buyer will question. That usually means the books, your own salary, any family members on payroll and the lease.

4. Build your team. Most sellers work with a broker and a dental attorney. We sit beside them on the financial and tax side.

5. Find the buyer. Your broker markets the practice, or you sell to an associate or partner you already know.

6. Negotiate the letter of intent. This is where the price, how it's split across the assets and how you'll be paid get agreed. It's the most important tax moment in the whole sale.

7. Get through the buyer's due diligence and financing. The buyer and their lender will go through your returns, P&Ls and production reports line by line.

8. Sign and close. The purchase agreement, the closing statement and the final allocation all get signed.

9. Hand over the practice. Many sellers stay on for a transition period to introduce patients and staff to the new owner.

10. File your last returns as an owner, and plan what to do with the proceeds.

How buyers and banks value a dental practice

Most buyers need a loan, so in practice your price is often capped by what a bank will lend against. Banks lend on cash flow. They want to see that the practice earns enough to pay a new owner a fair salary and still cover the loan.

So the starting point is profit, after a few adjustments. We replace whatever you've been paying yourself with a market-rate doctor's salary, add back one-off and personal expenses, and add in costs a new owner will have to carry. You'll also hear prices quoted as a percentage of collections. We look at both and explain where your practice sits and why.

Buyers look past the headline too. A full schedule only matters if the collections are there to match it. Steady revenue looks great until someone asks how many new patients come in, what you spend to get them and why revenue isn't growing. A good buyer will ask, so we ask first.

What lowers your sale price

Messy books are the most expensive mistake we see. Buyers bring us seller P&Ls that are hard to read and don't match the tax return. Clean books would have made many of those practices worth a lot more, and the seller rarely realizes it until the offers come in low.

Costs that look too good are another one. When a spouse runs the front office for a token wage, profit looks strong on paper. A buyer has to hire an office manager at a real wage, and their bank will adjust the profit down to match.

Other things that cost you: a lease with only a couple of years left and no renewal option, a practice where almost all the production depends on you, revenue that's flat while marketing spend keeps climbing, and owner pay that was never set at a reasonable salary.

What raises your sale price

Books closed every month and kept consistently, so three years of numbers tell one clear story.

A market-rate salary for you, so the profit a buyer sees is profit they'll actually keep.

A long lease with renewal options, or a clear plan for the building if you own it.

A strong hygiene department and steady new patients, so revenue doesn't depend only on your chair.

Profit that's been tracked and managed over time. Omar's view is simple: you never know when you'll need to sell, so watch your profit monthly, quarterly and yearly and stay in the driver's seat.

Who does what: broker, attorney and CPA

Your broker finds buyers, markets the practice and helps negotiate the price. Your dental attorney drafts and reviews the letter of intent, the purchase agreement and the lease assignment.

We handle the numbers and the taxes. We value the practice, prepare the financials a buyer will ask for, model your after-tax proceeds under different deal terms, plan how the price is split and how you're paid, and file your returns for the year of the sale.

Pricing a practice for the market is the broker's job. Making sure you understand what that price means after tax is ours. The best sales we see have all three working together from the letter of intent onward.

Asset sale or stock sale?

Most dental practice sales are asset sales. The buyer buys the practice's assets, like the equipment, the patient records and the goodwill, and usually sets up their own new entity to own them.

In a stock sale, the buyer buys your shares in the company itself. Stock sales are rare in dentistry, partly because buyers don't want to take on the old company's history. For the seller, a stock sale usually means a capital gain. Which structure fits your sale depends on your entity and the buyer, so it's worth planning before anyone agrees to terms.

Why the price split matters as much as the price

In an asset sale, the total price gets split across the assets being sold: equipment, supplies, goodwill and anything else in the deal. That split changes your tax bill as the seller, and it changes the buyer's write-offs for years.

Sellers usually want more of the price in goodwill, because goodwill you built yourself is generally taxed as a capital gain. Buyers usually want more in equipment, because they can write equipment off faster than goodwill, which they write off over 15 years. So the split is a negotiation, and it belongs in the letter of intent.

Equipment you've already depreciated adds another wrinkle. When you sell it for more than its remaining tax value, that gain can be taxed as ordinary income. The same applies to goodwill you bought and amortized when you first purchased the practice.

Both you and the buyer report the split to the IRS, and if you agree to it in writing, it binds you both. That's why we want to see the numbers before you sign, while there's still room to negotiate.

How you get paid, and what each option means for your taxes

Cash at closing is the simplest. The buyer's bank funds the deal and you're paid in full when it closes.

A seller note means you finance part of the price yourself and the buyer pays you over time. With an installment sale, the gain is generally spread across the years you're paid. Two catches: any depreciation recapture is taxed in the year of the sale even if you haven't received the cash yet, and the interest the buyer pays you is taxed as ordinary income. So a seller note can leave you with a tax bill in year one that's bigger than the cash you received in year one, and we plan for that up front.

An earnout ties part of the price to how the practice performs after the sale. It can close a gap between what you think the practice is worth and what the buyer will pay, but it also means part of your price depends on someone else running your practice.

Staying on as an associate after the sale is common, and it helps patients and staff move over to the new owner. That pay is for your work, taxed like any paycheck, and it's separate from the sale price.

A non-compete is standard in dental sales. Money paid to you for agreeing not to compete is generally taxed like pay, not as a capital gain, so how much of the price lands there matters too.

Selling to your associate or partner

Some of the best sales happen inside the practice. Your associate already knows the patients and the team, and the transition is smoother for everyone.

An internal sale needs the same valuation and tax planning as any other, plus a payment structure your associate can actually afford. That might mean a bank loan, a seller note, a buy-in over several years, or a mix. We model the options with both sides of the deal in mind, so the price is fair and the payments work.

Your walk-away number

The sale price is the number everyone talks about. The number that matters to you is what's left after the practice loan is paid off, the deal costs are paid and the taxes are paid.

So we start with three figures: what the practice is worth, what you owe, and what you need to retire or move on to whatever is next. If those three don't line up, it's much better to know with years to spare than weeks before closing.

Mistakes we see sellers make

Waiting too long to clean up the books, so the buyer's due diligence finds problems the seller never had time to fix.

Agreeing to a price in the letter of intent without agreeing how it's split, and finding out later that the split cost them more in tax than a lower price would have.

Accepting a seller note without planning for the tax due in the year of the sale.

Treating the sale as a broker's job alone, with nobody modeling the after-tax result of each offer.

Forgetting the lease. A buyer and their bank will want enough years left on it to cover the loan.

After the sale

Closing isn't the end of the paperwork. There's a final return for your practice entity, your personal return for the year of the sale, and any payments from a seller note to report in the years after.

It's also the moment to plan what the proceeds will do for you, whether that's retirement, real estate or the next practice. We help you put a plan around the money before it hits your account, so the tax side of the next step is set up as well as the sale was.

Questions we hear a lot

How is a dental practice valued for sale?

Usually off profit, after replacing the owner's pay with a market-rate salary and adding back one-off and personal costs. You'll also hear a percentage of collections. We look at both and show you where your practice sits.

How long does it take to sell a dental practice?

It depends on how ready the practice is, how quickly a buyer is found and how long their financing takes. The preparation is what you control, and it's worth starting years ahead.

Do I pay tax when I sell my dental practice?

Usually, yes. How much, and when, depends on how the price is split across the assets and how you're paid. That's why we plan it before the terms are set in the letter of intent.

Is selling the assets or the stock better?

Most dental sales are asset sales. Stock sales are rare in dentistry and usually mean a capital gain for the seller. The right structure depends on your entity and your buyer.

Should I offer seller financing?

It can make a sale possible and spread out your gain, but some of the tax can still be due in the year of the sale. We model it before you agree to it.

Can I keep working at the practice after I sell?

Yes, and many sellers do for a transition period. That pay is taxed like a paycheck and is separate from the sale price.

Do I still need a broker if I'm working with you?

Usually, yes. Your broker finds and negotiates with buyers. We handle the valuation, the numbers a buyer will ask for and the tax side of the deal.

Can you help me sell to my associate?

Yes. Internal sales need the same valuation and tax planning, plus a payment structure your associate can afford.
