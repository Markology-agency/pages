High Income, Thin Withholding, and a Trust Account

Solo and small-firm attorneys tell me the same things owners in every service business do. The accountant files the return but doesn't plan. The quarterly estimate shows up late, or it's a number with no explanation behind it. The S-corp was set up because someone said to, and nobody has checked whether the salary makes sense.

Then there's the part that's specific to law firms. Client money sits in a trust account, and it has to stay separate from the money the firm has earned. When the books blur the two, every report after that is wrong.

A CPA for a law firm has to get three things right.

what we handle

What We Handle for Law Firms

Law firm bookkeeping that respects the trust account

Operating and trust accounts kept separate in the books and reconciled every month, so client funds never show up as firm income.

Owner pay and the S-corp math

An S-corp can lower payroll tax, but only if the numbers work. The IRS expects the owner to take reasonable compensation, and a salary set too high defeats the purpose. We balance the salary against payroll tax, retirement funding, and the qualified business income deduction.

The plan for the year

A tax road map early in the year, quarterly estimated payments that match it, and a December projection while there's still time to act.

Questions Attorneys Ask

Should my law practice be an S-corp?
−

It depends on how much the practice earns after expenses and whether you already have a large W-2 from somewhere else. An S-corp can reduce self-employment tax, but it adds payroll, a separate return, and more bookkeeping. We run a projection before anyone files Form 2553.

How should trust accounts be handled in the books?
−

As their own accounts, completely separate from operating. Client money in trust isn't firm income. It's recorded and reconciled on its own, and only moves to operating when it's earned.

What deductions are available to a solo attorney?
−

Anything you spend to earn the income: bar dues, CLE, malpractice insurance, practice software, research tools, and office costs. A room at home used regularly and exclusively for the practice can qualify as a home office. Retirement plans for the self-employed, like a solo 401(k), are often the biggest lever.

Can you work with a firm in more than one state?
−

Yes. We figure out where the firm has to file based on where the work is done and where the income comes from, and build that into the plan from the start.
