Buying and selling

Why most dentists outgrow a generalist accountant

A generalist files your return, collects a fee and sees you again next spring. Nobody looks at your practice the way a buyer, a banker or another dentist would.

That gap shows up in a lot of places. We see full schedules with collections that never match them, overhead that creeps up a point a year because nobody watches the supply bill, and S-corp elections or owner salaries that were never set up properly.

We only work with dentists. We know what a healthy hygiene department looks like, how lab fees and supply costs move, how a bank reads a dental P&L and how a buyer will pick yours apart one day. So the advice you get from us fits a dental practice, at whatever stage yours is in.

Stage one: the 1099 associate

As a 1099 associate you're running a business, even if it doesn't feel like one yet. Nobody is withholding your taxes, nobody set up your retirement accounts, and every dollar you earn is exposed to self-employment tax until it has the right structure around it.

This is where we set up the basics: whether an entity makes sense for your income and your state, quarterly estimated payments so April brings no surprises, and a retirement plan that fits a high-earning year.

The ownership question starts here too. Most associates think about buying for years before they do it. We help you look at it with real numbers: what you'd need saved, what a lender will want to see from you, and what your take-home looks like as an owner compared with staying an associate.

Stage two: buying a practice

Buying a practice is the biggest financial decision most dentists make, and the numbers the seller shows you are rarely the numbers you'll live with.

Let's look at an example from our own client work. A doctor buying a practice got to sit with the seller and look at the schedule. It was completely full and booked out for weeks. Then we pulled the P&L, and the collections weren't there. A full schedule doesn't mean the money is being collected.

We see the other side of that too. A seller's spouse running the front office for a token wage makes the profit look great on paper. The day you buy it and hire an office manager at a real wage, that profit drops.

So before you sign the letter of intent, we rebuild the seller's numbers the way you'll actually run the practice. We review the last three years of tax returns and P&Ls, overhead, production by provider and patient retention. We put a market-rate doctor's salary in place of whatever the seller paid themselves, add back the one-off costs and add in the costs you'll have to carry.

Then we help with the money side. We work with dental-specific lenders every day, so we prepare the documents they want to see and help you compare offers. We also plan how the purchase is structured and how the price is split, because that affects your taxes for years after closing.

Your first year as an owner

The first year is where good habits get set or bad ones get baked in. We set up books built around how a dental practice earns and spends, payroll for your team, and the entity and owner salary decisions that are much easier to get right at the start.

We've had doctors who didn't elect S-corp status in year one of a startup, took a lot of deductions, then tried to switch in year two with the balance sheet out of line. Timing mistakes like that can turn into a large tax bill that nobody saw coming. The fix is a plan made before the first return, not after it.

Stage three: the established owner who is busy but not profitable

This is the most common call we get. Collections are good, the schedule is full, you're working hard and the take-home still isn't there.

When we look at a practice, we start with five numbers: collections, staff cost, variable costs like lab and supplies, fixed costs like rent, and the profit left at the end. Every problem in the practice shows up in one of those five. Your P&L works like an X-ray, and once you can read it you know where to treat.

Let's look at an example. An established practice came to us and we noticed their supply costs drop. We asked if they were buying less. They weren't. They'd been overpaying the same vendor for a year and a half, because they were busy growing and the rep brought cookies every six months. We set a bonus for the front desk tied to finding a better price, and they called vendors between patients. Nothing else changed, and the savings went straight to profit.

Sometimes the practice is profitable and the problem is cash. We had a client whose practice made money on paper but whose bank account never felt it, because too much was going to loan payments. We worked with a bank to refinance and combine the loans at a lower rate, and the cash flow opened up.

And sometimes the fix is a lot of small moves. Across many clients we've seen owners hold their margins while costs went up by adjusting a few points here and there: a fee here, a supply line there, a change in how they bring in new patients. Those small changes in the right numbers added up to a steady margin.

The numbers we track with you

Most practices track production and collections and stop there. We help you watch the numbers that actually drive profit: overhead as a percentage of collections, revenue per patient, hygiene reactivation, case acceptance and what it costs you to bring in a new patient.

Benchmarks depend on your stage. If you're a startup and your overhead looks terrible, it probably isn't, because you're building. A mature practice with the same overhead has a problem. We compare you to practices at your stage, not to an average that fits nobody.

Stage four: building wealth outside the practice

A profitable practice gives you options, and most owners never get asked what they want to do with them.

Let's look at an example. A client joined us with about a million dollars in collections and roughly $100,000 of take-home after taxes. Her schedule was full and her collections were solid. When we asked what success looked like for her, she said nobody had ever asked. She wanted to work three days a week and own two rental properties within four years. So we worked backward from the take-home she'd need, built the plan around that target and started tightening cash flow and tax strategy to get there.

This stage is where tax planning, retirement plans and real estate come together. We had a client whose landlord let their shopping center go downhill. When they ran the numbers on buying a standalone building nearby, the tax side of owning the building made the move worth it.

Stage five: preparing to sell, and selling

The best time to plan a sale is years before it, while there's still time to change the number. We start with three figures: what the practice is worth today, what you owe, and what you need to retire on. If the gap doesn't work, we know which levers to pull and how long it will take.

We see buyers come to us with seller P&Ls that are a mess, and the sad part is that clean books would have made those practices worth a lot more. Clean, consistent bookkeeping and a market-rate owner salary are two of the easiest things a seller can fix ahead of time.

When a buyer shows up, we work beside your broker and attorney on the financial side. We plan how the price is split and how you're paid before the terms are set, and we stay with you through closing and your last return as an owner.

What working with us looks like

We start with you, not the tax return. What do you want the practice to do for you, and by when?

Then we look at the numbers. Your P&L, tax returns, payroll and debt tell us where you are. We show you what we found in plain language and which two or three moves matter most.

Then we build the plan and stay on it with you every month: books closed on a set schedule, the KPIs that matter tracked against your goals, and a regular call to adjust as the year goes.

Questions we hear a lot

What does a dental practice consultant actually do?

It depends on the consultant. Practice management coaches usually work on systems, scripts and staff. We work from your P&L, tax returns and KPIs, so every recommendation ties back to a number you can track and a tax result you can plan for.

Do you only work with dentists?

Yes. Every client we serve is a dentist, from 1099 associates to multi-location owners.

I'm a 1099 associate. Is it too early to talk to you?

No. The decisions you make as an associate, like entity setup, retirement accounts and how much you save, decide how ready you are when the right practice comes along.

Can you help me decide whether a practice is worth buying?

Yes. We review the seller's last three years of returns and P&Ls, rebuild the profit the way you'll actually run it and tell you what we'd want answered before you sign.

My practice is busy. Why isn't it more profitable?

Usually it's one of five places: collections, staff cost, lab and supplies, fixed costs like rent, or what's left after debt and your own pay. We find which one first.

How does the consulting work?

It runs through our monthly advisory program. Each month we close your books, track your KPIs against your goals and meet with you to decide what to change next.
