Selling a dental practice: what it's worth and how the sale is taxed

Some owners plan a sale for years. Others get a family situation, a move or a bad back, and need to sell sooner than they thought. Either way, a buyer is going to judge your practice by its numbers, and you'll want to see those numbers the way they do first.

Preparing to sell

Why you watch your value before you plan to sell

We tell our monthly CFO clients to track EBITDA every month, because you never know if you'll need to sell the practice tomorrow. A family situation or a health problem doesn't wait until your books are ready.

It works like a rental property with peeling paint: you can still sell it, just for less. A practice that looks good to a bank and to a buyer keeps your door open when an opportunity, or a problem, shows up.

For most owners the practice is also the biggest asset they have. Raising its value moves your net worth more than almost anything you could do in the stock market.

How a buyer will read your numbers

We sit on the buyer's side of a lot of dental deals, so here's what a careful buyer will do with your P&L.

Most owners judge their practice by the production report and the bank balance, while a buyer starts with profit, which in dentistry usually means EBITDA. It's a good starting point, but we call it the first dirty version of the X-ray, and a careful buyer will want a cleaner image before they trust it.

Your own pay comes first. If you've paid yourself a low salary, the profit looks bigger than it really is. A buyer will gross your pay up to what it would cost to put an associate in your chair, and the profit drops to match.

The same goes for a spouse or family member running the front office for a token wage. A buyer will have to hire an office manager at a real wage, so they'll price that in. One-off spending works the other way: a heavy year of continuing education gets added back.

A full schedule only counts if the collections are there to match it. If you bring in plenty of new patients and spend real money on marketing while revenue stays flat, a buyer will ask where those patients went.

Two things that drag the price down

The first is messy books. We see buyers bring us seller P&Ls that were done incorrectly, with expenses in the wrong place, personal costs mixed in and numbers that don't show a true business. The sad part is that cleaner books would have made a lot of those practices worth more, because a buyer can only pay for profit they can see.

Clean monthly books are one of the easiest things to fix years ahead of a sale, and keeping them that way is what our monthly dental bookkeeping is for.

The second is the lease. Wages, marketing and lab costs can all be managed, but rent mostly can't, and it tends to go up. A high lease on a smaller practice makes it hard to sell, and it's a combination we tell first-time buyers to walk away from.

Years left on the lease matter too. If you're seven years into a ten-year lease with no renewal rights, a buyer sees three years left, and that's not long enough to move a practice without hurting it. It's worth talking to your landlord about extension options well before you go to market.

Asset sale or stock sale?

Most dental practice sales are asset sales. The buyer brings an empty box, usually a new entity of their own, and buys the pieces: the equipment, the supplies, the patient records and the goodwill. Your old entity is left empty and gets closed.

In a stock sale the buyer takes your whole company, history and all. We rarely see these in dentistry, partly because the buyer takes on the old entity's liabilities and gets no fresh write-offs. For the seller, selling stock usually produces a capital gain. Which structure fits depends on your entity and your buyer, so settle it before anyone agrees to terms.

We compare the two in more detail in our post on asset sale vs stock sale of a dental practice.

How the price gets split, and why it changes your tax

In an asset sale, the total price is split line by line across what's being sold, like an itemized receipt. Equipment and supplies sit on one side, and patient records, goodwill and the non-compete sit on the other.

The split matters to both of you, so it's a negotiation. Sellers generally want more of the price in goodwill, which is usually taxed as a capital gain. Buyers generally want more in equipment, because they can write equipment off faster, while goodwill is written off over 15 years.

From the buyer's side, we see a lot of deals where the buyer had no real guidance on the split. A buyer who is well advised will push back on a split that puts nearly everything into goodwill, so expect it to be part of the conversation.

Watch the equipment you've already depreciated. If you sell it for more than its remaining tax value, that gain can be taxed as ordinary income. The same goes for goodwill you bought and amortized when you first purchased the practice.

Both of you have to follow the same IRS method for splitting the price, and both of you report the split on Form 8594. If you agree to the split in writing, it binds you both, so it's much easier to shape before you sign than after. We go deeper on this from the buyer's side in our post on goodwill allocation when buying a dental practice.

How you get paid, and what each option means for your taxes

Cash at closing is the simplest. The buyer's bank funds the loan and you're paid when the deal closes.

With a seller note, you finance part of the price yourself and the buyer pays you over time. With an installment sale, the gain is generally reported as you receive the payments. The catch is that any depreciation recapture is taxed in the year of the sale, and the interest the buyer pays you is ordinary income. A seller note can leave you with a first-year tax bill larger than the cash you received that year, so plan for it before you agree.

Money paid to you for a non-compete is generally taxed like pay rather than as a capital gain. If you stay on to work at the practice after the sale, that pay is for your work, taxed like any paycheck and separate from the sale price. State tax on all of this varies by state.

We explain the seller note option in more detail in our post on selling a dental practice on an installment sale.

Questions we hear a lot

How is a dental practice valued for sale?

Usually from profit, after a few adjustments: your own pay set to what an associate would cost, one-off and personal expenses added back, and costs a new owner will carry added in. You'll also hear prices quoted as a percentage of collections. Those vary a lot by practice and area, so we'd rather look at your numbers than quote a range.

Do I pay tax when I sell my dental practice?

Usually, yes. How much, and when, depends on how the price is split across the assets, how you're paid and your state. That's why the split and the payment terms are worth planning before you sign.

Should I offer seller financing?

It can make a sale possible and spread your gain over the years you're paid. Depreciation recapture is still taxed in the year of the sale, though, so make sure you have the cash for that bill.

What should I do first if I'm thinking about selling?

Find out what the practice is worth today. Our free valuation calculator gives you a rough range, and a written valuation gives you a number you can plan around.
