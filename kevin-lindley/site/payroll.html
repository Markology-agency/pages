Questions We Answer Before the First Payroll

Say you just elected S-corp status and your first payroll is coming up. Before anything runs, these are the questions we work through with you.

Does your entity require owner payroll?

A sole proprietor doesn't run payroll for themselves. Once you elect S-corp status and work in the business, you're an employee of your own company, so you need to pay yourself a salary through payroll.

What's a reasonable salary for you?

We look at your role, your field, what the business earns and how much you want to put toward retirement. Your retirement contributions are tied to that salary, so we set the two together.

Which payroll provider should you use?

We usually set clients up on Gusto. It's affordable, and it splits each paycheck between you, the IRS and the state and files the payroll forms. If you already have a provider that works, we can keep it.

What changes when you hire someone, pay your spouse or work in another state?

Each one can mean a new registration, a different withholding setup or a new state account. We check what's needed before the first paycheck goes out.

Can a missed or short payroll be fixed before year end?

Usually, yes. If payroll started late or the salary for the year came in too low, we can catch it up with payroll runs before December 31, so the W-2 shows the full year. It's much easier to fix in the fall than after the year closes, which is one more reason we do a December projection.

Once the owner's payroll is set, the next question we hear is about the family.

family payroll

Paying Family Members Through the Business

If your kids do real work for the business, paying them can be a legitimate strategy, and their earnings can go toward a Roth IRA in their name.

It needs a real job, a reasonable wage for that job, and records to back it up. The IRS rules for family employees differ by entity type, so we set it up based on how your business is structured.

Say you have a teenager who scans receipts, files paperwork or helps with your social media. If the work is real and the pay is in line with what you'd pay someone else for that job, they can be on payroll like any other employee. You get a deduction for what you pay them, and the wages give them the earned income they need to put money into a Roth IRA.

We help you set up a simple record of the job: what they do, the hours they work and the rate. Their pay comes from the business account, so there's a clear paper trail if anyone ever asks.

How the wages are taxed depends on your entity. A child under 18 working for a parent's sole proprietorship, or a partnership owned only by the parents, isn't subject to Social Security and Medicare tax. A child working for a corporation is, at any age. We confirm the setup before the first payment, so there's no surprise on the payroll forms.

A spouse or another family member can work in the business too. How much to pay a spouse, or whether to pay one at all, is part of the same conversation we have about your own salary and retirement funding. We'll show you the numbers both ways before you decide.

Payroll Questions We Get Asked

What counts as a reasonable salary for an S-corp owner?
−

The IRS requires S-corp owners to take reasonable compensation for the work they do before taking distributions. There's no single formula. We look at your role, your profession, what the business earns, and how much you want to put into retirement, then set a salary that fits the facts and that I'm comfortable defending.

Can I pay my kids through the business?
−

Yes, if they do real work and the pay is reasonable for that work. The rules for family employees differ depending on whether your business is a sole proprietorship, a partnership, or a corporation. We set it up to match your entity and keep the records to support it.

Does it matter if there's no payroll set up yet when electing S-corp status?
−

It's not ideal, but it's fixable. A W-2 is an annual form, so payroll can technically catch up later in the year. I'd still rather have payroll running well before year end, so there's working capital in the business and the salary isn't one big December catch-up. We usually file Form 2553 and set up payroll together.

How do I get paid out of my S-corp?
−

Two ways. A salary through payroll is subject to income tax and payroll taxes. Distributions are subject to income tax only. The salary has to be reasonable first, then the rest can come out as distributions. We plan that split every year.
