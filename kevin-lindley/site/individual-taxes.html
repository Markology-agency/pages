The W-2 Was Easy. Then the Second Income Showed Up.

Coming out of training, half of every paycheck seemed to disappear, and nobody explained why.

Then the side income started. It might be talks, expert witness work, locums shifts, or a telehealth contract. None of it has withholding, and it's hard to plan for because it doesn't arrive on a schedule.

You file, the number is higher than last year, and you wonder if you did something wrong. You know the strategies exist. You're just not sure which ones apply to you or how to actually carry them out.

Here's what changes when someone plans the whole household.

One Plan for Both Incomes

Withholding and estimates, together

We look at both W-2 paystubs and the side income at the same time. You can cover the side income with quarterly estimated payments, or by raising your W-2 withholding instead. For a lot of households, the withholding route is simpler.

Honest about the S-corp

With a large W-2 already in place, an S-corp on the side income often costs more in setup, payroll, and filings than it saves. We run the math before anyone files anything.

The deductions that are yours

Anything you spend to earn the side income, like licensing, continuing education, travel, and the phone and laptop you use for the work. Add a home office if you have a dedicated space, and the vehicle where the business use is real.

Retirement Accounts for Self-Employed Income

The biggest benefit of self-employed income is how much more you can put toward retirement. Plans like a solo 401(k) or a SEP IRA open up room a W-2 alone doesn't give you. The IRS summarizes the options for self-employed retirement plans.

Which plan fits depends on your income, your staff, and whether you're also doing a backdoor Roth. A SEP IRA can get in the way of a backdoor Roth, so we sort that out before you open anything.

I also like clients to keep money in more than one bucket: some pre-tax, some Roth, and some in a regular taxable account. The regular account gets underrated. It's the one you can reach without a penalty when life changes.

Questions High Earners Ask

Should I be worried about a large refund?+

Not from an audit standpoint. A big refund on its own doesn't flag anything. It usually means you paid in more than you needed to during the year, so we adjust the withholding to land closer to even next year.

What changes if I go from W-2 to 1099?+

Nobody withholds for you anymore, so you'll make estimated payments or cover it through a spouse's withholding. You also pay both halves of Social Security and Medicare through self-employment tax, and you lose employer benefits like health insurance and the 401(k) match. A 1099 offer needs to pay meaningfully more than the W-2 just to come out even. The upside is that you can deduct what it costs to earn the income and save far more for retirement.

Should we file jointly if one spouse earns much more?+

Usually, yes. I've seen several couples file separately because they thought they had to, and it cost them. I can run both filing statuses side by side and show you the difference before we file. If you already filed separately, it can often be amended.

Can I deduct the tax prep fee against the 1099 income?+

The part of the fee for preparing the business side of your return, yes. IRS Publication 334 allows that on Schedule C. It's deducted in the year you pay it, not the year the return covers.
