What an S-Corp election actually changes.

An S-Corp is not a new entity. Your LLC stays an LLC. The S-Corp election is a different way of telling the IRS how to tax the LLC you already have, and for a business that has grown past the point where the self-employment tax really starts to sting, it can drop your federal tax bill by a meaningful chunk.

The mechanic is simple. Without the election, every dollar of profit your LLC earns is treated as self-employment income and runs through self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare combined). With the election, you split the same profit into two buckets: a reasonable salary paid through payroll, and a distribution. Payroll taxes apply to the salary. The distribution side avoids self-employment tax entirely. That second bucket is where the savings live.

Let us look at an example. Say your business clears $250,000 in profit for the year. Taxed as a regular LLC, the self-employment tax alone is roughly $30,000. Take the S-Corp election, pay yourself a salary of $110,000, and run the other $140,000 as a distribution. The payroll taxes on the salary land around $17,000, which is real money back in your pocket without changing what you actually do day to day.

One thing worth saying out loud: this is an estimate, not a tax return. The math assumes you qualify for the standard FUTA state credit (0.6% effective rate on the first $7,000 of wages), which is the case for most Illinois employers in good standing on state unemployment. It does not model state unemployment insurance, the §199A qualified business income deduction (the S-Corp election can shrink that deduction, which sometimes offsets part of the savings), workers' compensation, or your federal and state income tax. The salary range we suggest is a heuristic. The IRS reasonable compensation standard is fact-based and depends on your role, hours, and what people in your industry actually earn for the same work.
