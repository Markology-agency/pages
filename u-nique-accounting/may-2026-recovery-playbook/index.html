Full body · paste in ElementorIf you own a restaurant, the P&L is the document that tells you whether last month worked. Not the bank account. Not the POS sales report. The P&L. And if yours is set up like a generic small-business P&L, you're missing the numbers that actually matter in restaurants. We'll walk you through what a real restaurant P&L should look like, what every line item is telling you, and where most restaurant P&Ls go wrong. ## What Is a Restaurant P&L? A restaurant profit and loss statement is a monthly summary of every dollar that came in and every dollar that went out, organized the way hospitality businesses actually run. Generic P&Ls lump cost of goods sold into one number. Restaurant P&Ls break it apart, because the difference between a 32% food cost and a 38% food cost is a profitable month or a panic month, and you can't see that gap on a generic chart of accounts. ## What Goes On a Restaurant P&L? A clean restaurant P&L has five sections in this order: 1. **Sales** — broken into food, beverage (alcohol), and other (delivery fees, merch, catering) 2. **Cost of Goods Sold** — food cost and beverage cost calculated separately 3. **Labor** — split between BOH (back of house), FOH (front of house), and salaried management 4. **Operating Expenses** — rent, utilities, royalties, supplies, marketing, repairs 5. **Net Income** — what's actually left The two summary numbers underneath that owners check first: **gross margin** (Sales minus COGS) and **prime cost** (COGS + Labor, expressed as % of sales). If you only watch one number monthly, watch prime cost. ## What Is a Good Prime Cost for a Restaurant? Most full-service restaurants should be running prime cost at 60-65% of sales. Quick-service can push lower, around 55-60%. Fine dining often runs higher, 65-68%. If you're above 70%, the model is broken and no amount of marketing will fix it — the math just doesn't work. Let's look at an example. A casual full-service restaurant we work with was running 73% prime cost going into 2025. They had a packed dining room every weekend and a strong reputation. They were also losing money. We pulled apart the prime cost into the two components: food was 34% (high but not catastrophic) and labor was 39% (way too high). The fix wasn't marketing or menu price increases. It was rebuilding the schedule against actual cover counts by daypart. Three months later prime cost was 64%. ## How Often Should You Look at a Restaurant P&L? Monthly, minimum. Weekly is better for prime cost specifically. The monthly P&L is for full-picture decisions: pricing, leases, capital purchases, owner draw. The weekly view is for operational decisions: schedule corrections, menu mix adjustments, vendor pricing fights. Most independent operators we onboard don't have either rhythm in place. That's the first thing we fix. ## What's the Difference Between Food Cost % and Prime Cost? Food cost % is just one piece of prime cost. Food cost = total food purchases divided by food sales. Prime cost = food cost + labor cost, divided by total sales. A 30% food cost on its own tells you almost nothing. A 30% food cost combined with 32% labor cost tells you everything: 62% prime cost, healthy restaurant. Most P&L reviews we run with new clients start with the owner watching food cost like a hawk while labor drifted up unnoticed. The two numbers move together, and you need to watch them together. ## Why Most Restaurant P&Ls Don't Work Three patterns we see over and over: **Pattern 1: Generic accounting software, generic chart of accounts.** QuickBooks Online out of the box doesn't know your business. If your CPA set you up with a one-line "cost of goods" account, your food and beverage cost is invisible. You need a hospitality-specific chart of accounts, with food cost broken into produce / protein / dairy / dry goods at minimum, and beverage cost broken into liquor / beer / wine / NA. **Pattern 2: Labor is one line.** Lumping kitchen labor with server labor with management salary tells you nothing. If you can't see BOH labor as a % of food sales and FOH labor as a % of total sales, you can't make staffing decisions. **Pattern 3: Monthly close runs 30+ days behind.** A P&L for March that shows up in May is a historical artifact. You can't act on it. Monthly close should be done by the 10th of the following month, every month, no exceptions. ## How Do You Calculate Restaurant Profit Margin? Net profit margin = (Net Income / Total Sales) × 100. For most full-service restaurants, a healthy net margin sits between 6% and 12%. Quick-service can hit 15-20% in mature units. Independent fine dining often runs at 3-5% because the cost structure is so much higher. The path to that net margin is not magic. It's prime cost control plus disciplined operating expense management. We set most of our restaurant clients up on Xero with Syft (https://u-niqueaccounting.com/syft/) on top, which gives them a monthly P&L view where prime cost, gross margin, and net margin all live on one page — not a pile of separate reports. ## What's the Best Restaurant P&L Template? The best template is the one your accountant is also using, because the alternative is a Frankenstein P&L that breaks every time you compare months. The U-Nique team uses a hospitality chart of accounts that maps directly to our reporting layer. If you're DIY and want a starting point, we publish a free downloadable P&L template at https://u-niqueaccounting.com/restaurant-chart-of-accounts-2025/. ## When Should You Hire a Restaurant Accountant? If any of these are true: prime cost is above 65% and you don't know which side of it is bleeding, your monthly close is more than 30 days behind, you've never seen a P&L broken out by daypart, or your CPA can't explain FICA tip credit to you in plain language. We focus exclusively on restaurants and breweries (https://u-niqueaccounting.com/restaurant-accountants/) because the details matter here in a way they don't in generic small business accounting. If your current accountant doesn't specialize in hospitality, the P&L isn't going to get cleaner on its own. Full body · paste in ElementorIf you're opening a restaurant or already running one, the question of LLC versus S Corp comes up early. Usually from your attorney, sometimes from your accountant, occasionally from a friend who read something online. The answer matters more than most owners realize, because the wrong structure can cost you tens of thousands a year in unnecessary self-employment tax — and the right structure can quietly save you that same amount, every single year. We'll walk you through how we actually advise restaurant clients on this. Not the textbook version. The real one. ## What's the Difference Between an LLC and an S Corp? An LLC is a legal structure. An S Corp is a tax election. You can be both — and most restaurant owners we work with end up being both, an LLC that has elected S Corp status with the IRS. The legal side (LLC) governs how you're organized, how you handle liability, how members are treated. The tax side (S Corp election) governs how you pay yourself and how your business income is taxed. ## Why Do Restaurants Default to an LLC? Most independent restaurants open as a single-member LLC or a multi-member LLC. Three reasons: it's cheap to set up, it shields personal assets from business liability, and it's simple to administer in year one when you're just trying to keep the doors open. But by default, an LLC's income flows to your personal tax return as self-employment income. That means every dollar of profit is subject to 15.3% self-employment tax (Social Security + Medicare) on top of regular income tax. For a profitable restaurant clearing $150,000 in owner income, that's roughly $20,000-$23,000 a year just in self-employment tax. ## When Should a Restaurant Elect S Corp Status? The rule of thumb we use: once net business income consistently runs over $50,000-$60,000 a year, the S Corp election starts paying for itself. Below that, the cost and complexity of running payroll for yourself (which the S Corp election requires) outweighs the tax savings. Let's look at an example. A two-location independent restaurant came to us in 2024 still operating as a default LLC. Owner was pulling about $180,000 a year in net business income, paying around $27,000 in self-employment tax. We made the S Corp election effective for the next tax year. They went on payroll at a $75,000 reasonable-comp salary (which is subject to payroll tax) and took the remaining $105,000 as distributions (which are not subject to self-employment tax). Net savings: about $16,000 a year. Annual cost of compliance (additional payroll runs, separate tax return): about $2,800. Real-world annual benefit: $13,000+, every year. ## How Does the S Corp Election Actually Work? You file IRS Form 2553 to elect S Corp status. If you want it effective for the current tax year, you generally need to file within 75 days of formation (for a new entity) or within the first 2.5 months of the tax year (for an existing entity). Late elections are possible but require a reasonable-cause explanation. After the election, you have to: 1. **Put yourself on payroll** at a reasonable salary 2. **Run actual payroll** through a real payroll provider, not just write yourself a check 3. **Separate salary (W-2 wages) from distributions** clearly in the books 4. **File Form 1120-S** for the business each year, in addition to your personal return The "reasonable salary" requirement is what trips up most operators. The IRS doesn't publish a number — they expect you to pay yourself what you'd pay an unrelated employee in the same role. For a restaurant owner who's also functioning as the GM, that's typically $60,000-$90,000 depending on market and concept size. ## What's a "Reasonable Salary" for a Restaurant Owner? Look at three benchmarks: what a hired GM would earn in your market for your concept, what your salaried managers are paid, and what the BLS reports for "food service managers" in your metro area. If you pay yourself $30,000 and take $150,000 in distributions, the IRS will reclassify the distributions as wages and bill you for the back payroll tax plus penalties. We've seen this exact audit happen. Don't underpay. ## Does the S Corp Election Affect Your Restaurant Payroll? It changes how your owner pay is processed but doesn't affect employee payroll. Your servers, cooks, and managers run through payroll exactly the same way they always have. The only addition is a payroll run for you (the owner) at your reasonable-comp salary. That said, most operators we onboard end up cleaning up employee payroll at the same time, because the act of putting yourself on payroll forces a closer look at the system. FICA tip credit setup, tip pooling vs tip sharing accounting, and overtime calculations on tipped employees are all places we routinely find errors during S Corp transitions. The free FICA tip credit calculator at https://u-niqueaccounting.com/fica-tip-credit-calculator/ is the fastest way to see if you're leaving credit money on the table. ## When Does an S Corp NOT Make Sense for a Restaurant? Three situations: **You're a single-owner concept clearing under $60K net.** The compliance cost erodes the savings. Stay LLC, revisit annually. **You have multiple non-resident or non-individual owners.** S Corps have strict shareholder rules. Foreign investors, trusts, and corporate owners are usually disqualifying. **You're planning to bring on venture investors or a corporate buyer.** S Corp restrictions don't play well with institutional capital. Most exit-track restaurant groups end up converting to C Corp or partnership-taxed LLC before raising or selling. ## How Do You Switch From LLC to S Corp? You don't actually switch the legal entity. The LLC stays an LLC. You file the S Corp election (Form 2553) which changes how the IRS taxes the LLC. Most of our restaurant clients keep their LLC name and operating agreement unchanged. The only thing that changes is the tax treatment. The mechanics: file Form 2553 within the window, get a payroll provider set up before the effective date, and have your accountant build the owner-comp budget into the operating cash flow forecast so the salary runs don't surprise you. ## Should a Multi-Location Restaurant Group Use S Corp? Generally yes, up to a point. Most regional restaurant groups we work with run each location as a separately-formed LLC (for liability isolation) and either elect S Corp on each entity or on a parent holding entity that takes management fees from the operating LLCs. The optimal structure depends on ownership splits, debt structure, and exit plans. If you're considering opening a second location, talk to a hospitality CPA about structure before you sign the lease, not after. ## What Else Does the S Corp Election Affect? Two practical impacts most restaurant operators don't think about: **Health insurance:** S Corp owners with more than 2% of shares can't pay personal health insurance through the business pre-tax in the normal way. There's a workaround (the 2% shareholder health insurance rule) that requires running premiums through payroll. Your payroll provider needs to know about this. **Retirement contributions:** SEP-IRA, Solo 401(k), and SIMPLE IRA contribution limits are calculated differently for S Corp owners than for sole proprietors. The math is more favorable for S Corp owners at higher income levels. We help restaurant clients model the full picture before pulling the trigger. The savings are real, but only if the rest of the financial setup supports the structure. If you're trying to figure out whether the S Corp election makes sense for your concept, talk to us. We'll run the math on your actual P&L and tell you what the move would save (or cost). Restaurant accounting (https://u-niqueaccounting.com/restaurant-accountants/) is the only thing we do, so the answer comes from looking at hundreds of restaurant P&Ls, not from a tax textbook. Want to talk through your specific setup? https://u-niqueaccounting.com/contact-us/ Until next time!
