Buying a practice

It's the biggest purchase most dentists make in their lifetime, and it carries the highest opportunity cost. So when you're looking at one, we sit down with you for about an hour on the business side of the practice. We go through the seller's numbers and the questions you should ask the seller, and see whether the practice matches what you want from an office.

The question we always ask a buyer

If you bought this practice, put an associate in the chair and went to Hawaii for four years, would there be money in the bank when you came back? If the answer is yes, you bought a business.

If the practice only works when you're the one producing every day, we call that golden handcuffs. You paid for a job you can't step away from.

What we look for in the seller's numbers

Owner pay comes first. We back out whatever the seller pays themselves and put in what you'd pay an associate to do that production. The profit left after that swap is what the practice really earns. We don't price a practice off the broker's doctor's discretionary income figure, because it assumes you're the one producing.

EBITDA is a good starting point, but we call it the first dirty version of the X-ray. A seller paying themselves a low salary can make it look like a smash hit. One-off spending, like a big year of continuing education, can push it the other way.

Higher production doesn't always mean higher profit. Let's look at an example. A doctor we were working with got to sit in the office he was buying and look at the schedule, and it was full and booked out for weeks, so he was excited. Then we started looking at the P&L and asked where the money was, because the collections weren't there.

We've also seen practices where the seller's spouse is the office manager at a token wage. The numbers look great on paper until you buy the practice and hire an office manager at a real wage, and then the profit drops.

We look at new patients against revenue as well. If a practice brings in plenty of new patients and spends real money on marketing while revenue stays flat, patients are leaving out the back door.

And when an owner with several offices sells one so they can focus on the others, we check whether lab, supplies or staff for the office for sale are being paid through the ones they're keeping. The P&L can look lean for the wrong reason.

The lease, the upgrades and the bank

Wages, marketing and lab costs can all be managed after you buy. Rent mostly can't, and it tends to go up. When a smaller practice carries a heavy lease, our advice to a first-time owner is usually to walk away, because that lease will also make the practice hard to sell later.

Then add up what it will really cost. Digitizing paper charts, new equipment, leasehold improvements and signage all sit on top of the purchase price. Buyers get emotional about an office and forget those pieces, and the total can turn a good price into golden handcuffs.

We often hear that the bank approved it, so it must be a good practice. The bank is checking whether you can pay the loan back. That's a good first box to tick, and you still have to look a lot deeper than that.

How the purchase price gets split

Think of it like a Costco receipt. The bill of sale breaks the price down line by line into equipment, supplies, patient records, goodwill and the non-compete, and that breakdown drives your deductions for years. Goodwill is written off over 15 years, while equipment is generally written off faster.

A lot of the buyers we see have no idea about the allocation. They know it was discussed and some numbers were mentioned, but nobody guided them on it. Without advice, the seller tends to put most of the price into goodwill and patient records, which gives you the slowest write-off.

Pushing it too far the other way has limits. If the practice has real revenue and a name in the neighborhood, a split that's almost all equipment won't reflect what you bought. We wrote more about this in our post on goodwill allocation when buying a dental practice.

Most dental purchases are asset purchases. You come with an empty box, usually a new entity of your own, and buy the pieces. We almost never recommend buying the seller's stock, because you take on the old company's liabilities and get no fresh write-offs.

Your first year as the owner

Your LLC is your legal structure, and an S-corp is a tax election on top of it. The timing of that election matters a lot after a purchase, so we usually decide it once the first year's numbers are in.

Thinking about selling, now or one day

We tell our monthly CFO clients to watch EBITDA every month, because you never know if you'll need to sell the practice tomorrow. A family situation or a move won't wait until your books are ready.

For owners thinking about a sale, we offer a written valuation that answers the first question every seller asks: what should my practice sell for? From there you can decide whether to sell now or spend a few years making the practice worth more.

The things that drag the price down are the same ones we check for buyers: messy books, an owner salary that hides the real profit, and a heavy lease or one with only a few years left. All of them are easier to fix years ahead than months ahead.

What is my practice worth?

We get this question all the time. Most doctors have heard the word EBITDA, and fewer are sure what's inside it or what it hides.

Most owners judge their practice by the production report and the bank statement, while the market judges it by profit. Your practice is probably your biggest asset, and raising its value moves your net worth more than almost anything you could do in the stock market.

Questions we hear a lot

When should I bring you in on a purchase?

As early as you can, ideally while you're still asking the seller questions. Once the letter of intent is signed, the price and terms are mostly agreed, and there's less left to change. We cover that stage in our post on letter of intent negotiation for dentists buying a practice.

The bank approved my loan. Doesn't that mean it's a good deal?

It means the bank thinks you can pay the loan back. It doesn't tell you whether the profit is real once a market salary goes in, or whether you could sell the practice later.

Should I buy the seller's stock or the assets?

In dentistry it's almost always the assets. Buying the stock means taking on the old company's liabilities and starting with no fresh write-offs.

I'm years away from selling. Is it too early to think about value?

No. You never know when you might need to sell, and the things that raise a practice's value, like clean books, a market salary for you and a sensible lease, take a few years to show up in the numbers a buyer looks at.
