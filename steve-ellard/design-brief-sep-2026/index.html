Read this first: two brands, not one

This campaign runs three ads under two different names, and getting them mixed up is the one mistake that would make the whole set unusable.

Atlantic Payroll is Steve's own payroll service line, not a separate company and not a partner. It lives on his website and the page is titled "Cape Cod Payroll Services | Atlantic Payroll by Ellard CPA", so the two names already appear together in market. Concept 1 leads with Atlantic Payroll. Concepts 2 and 3 lead with Steve Ellard CPA and don't mention payroll at all.

Open question for concept 1: there doesn't appear to be a separate Atlantic Payroll logo file on the site, only content images. Check with Whitney whether one exists. If it doesn't, set "Atlantic Payroll" as a wordmark in the heading font with the Ellard CPA logo smaller alongside it, matching the "by Ellard CPA" relationship the site already uses. Don't invent a logo mark.

The client in one paragraph

Steven M. Ellard CPA is a local CPA firm on Cape Cod, Massachusetts. Steve is the neighbourhood accountant, not a national brand, and the whole positioning rests on being local, reachable and proactive. The clients are year-round Cape business owners and seasonal operators: contractors, restaurants, retail, plus Cape individuals with a rental or seasonal income wrinkle. The tone is warm and dry-witted, never corporate and never salesy.

The single most important visual instruction: this has to look like it came from a firm on the Cape, not from a stock-photo template. If the creative could be swapped onto any accounting firm in any state without anyone noticing, it hasn't done its job.

Rules that apply to all nine

  • Text on the image stays short. The primary text does the talking; the image is not where the

argument gets made. Aim for six words or fewer on the frame.

  • Keep overlay text off the bottom-right corner, where Meta stacks the CTA button on some

placements.

  • No stock photography of handshakes, skylines, suited people at conference tables, or anyone

pointing at a laptop. It reads as generic and it kills the local claim.

  • No fake data. No invented dashboards, chart mockups with made-up numbers, or screenshots of

software the firm doesn't use.

  • No badges, seals or ribbon graphics implying an award or certification that hasn't been earned.
  • Nothing implying a guaranteed outcome, a refund amount, or a savings figure. This matters most

on concept 2, where the whole idea sits near a savings claim. The copy has been written carefully to stay on the right side of that line, so the design must not put a number back in. No dollar signs, no percentages, no up-and-to-the-right arrows.

  • Accessible contrast. Slate on off-white is fine; red on slate is not.

Concept 1: Payroll and the seasonal headcount swing

Brand: Atlantic Payroll On-frame text: Twelve in June. Three in October. Feeling: recognition, with the dry humour doing the work. Every seasonal operator on the Cape has lived this and nobody has ever seen it named in an ad.

Visual direction: the headcount swing is the whole idea, so make it visual rather than decorative. A simple typographic or iconographic treatment of many-to-few reads instantly at feed size: a row of figures thinning out, a number dropping, a roster shortening. Keep it schematic and confident, not cute or infographic-heavy.

This is the one concept where the brand lockup needs the most care, because it introduces a name most viewers won't have seen. Give "Atlantic Payroll" real presence in the frame rather than tucking it in a corner.

Avoid: photos of people at a payroll terminal, timesheets, generic office admin imagery.

Concept 2: What actually changes an April tax bill

Brand: Steve Ellard CPA On-frame text: A surprise in April starts in September Feeling: calm and a little pointed. It's making an argument about how the work should be done, so it should feel considered rather than urgent.

Visual direction: the contrast between two points in the year is the idea. A restrained typographic treatment that sets September against April, or a calendar-year device where the useful window sits months before the visible deadline, will do more than any illustration. Slate type on the off-white ground, red used once, on the word that carries the turn.

Read the copy rules above again before starting this one. No numbers, no currency, no charts, no arrows pointing up. The moment a figure appears on this frame it becomes a savings claim and the ad can't run.

Avoid: calendars with a date circled in red marker, hourglasses, stress imagery, anything that turns a planning message into a deadline message. The deadline ad was deliberately cut from this campaign and this creative must not quietly become one.

Concept 3: Off-season cleanup

Brand: Steve Ellard CPA On-frame text: The quiet season is planning season Feeling: the most distinctly Cape Cod of the three, and the one where local texture earns its place.

Visual direction: this is where an authentic off-season Cape image belongs. An empty harbour, a closed-up seasonal storefront, quiet water, a Main Street without the summer crowd. Shot in the grey-blue and sand tones the Cape actually has in autumn, not a bright summer postcard. The point is the contrast between the season that just ended and the quiet stretch that's opened up.

If a licensed image of the actual Cape can be sourced, use it. If not, a typographic treatment in those same muted tones beats a generic coastal stock shot from somewhere else. A recognisably wrong location does more damage than no photo at all.

Whitney marked this concept "maybe", so if something has to give on time, this is the one to hand back last.

Handback
