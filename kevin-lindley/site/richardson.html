Going Self-Employed Is Harder Than Anyone Says

The first check arrives with nothing taken out. Contracts come in unevenly, so one month is great and the next is quiet. Someone told you to set aside 40%, and you're not sure that's right for you.

You're also doing the bookkeeping in your head, or in a folder of receipts. That feels safe, but it's the weakest record you can have.

None of this means you're doing it wrong. You're early. It takes a few years before self-employment feels routine, and the first year always feels like a lot.

Setting Up Self-Employed Income in Richardson

1. A dedicated business account

Every payment goes in and every business expense comes out of one account. If you're ever audited, that account is where the IRS starts. If your business runs through your personal account, they'll want to look through every transaction in it. It's like airport security digging through your whole bag.

2. A simple set-aside

A percentage of each payment goes into a separate savings account for taxes. We set your percentage in the tax road map. It starts a little high, so December is about what to do with the extra.

3. Quarterly payments that follow the income

We take what came in last quarter and apply your rate. Then you send the estimated payment. When the contracts are uneven, the payments are uneven too, and that's fine.

4. The S-corp, when it's worth it

Not before. An S-corp lowers self-employment tax. It also adds payroll, a separate return, and a reasonable salary requirement. The savings have to clear that cost comfortably before we file Form 2553.

5. Retirement that fits the rest of the plan

A solo 401(k) or a SEP IRA. I usually lean toward the solo 401(k), because a SEP IRA balance gets in the way of a backdoor Roth.

The Business Side, Handled

Once it's set up, it mostly runs itself. You send a payment to savings, send the quarterly estimate, and text me when something changes. A new contract, a bigger client, or a slow quarter all count.

In December we look at January through November, estimate December, and build a draft return. That's when we decide on the last retirement contribution, whether a purchase makes sense this year, and whether the S-corp is worth it for next year.

Questions Richardson Consultants Ask

How much should I set aside for taxes?+

It depends on your deductions, your retirement contributions, and whether another state is involved, so I won't give everyone the same number. You get your own percentage in the road map, and it changes as your deductions and contracts change. The December projection then tells us exactly what the last payment should be.

Solo 401(k) or SEP IRA?+

For most self-employed professionals, I prefer the solo 401(k). Money sitting in a SEP IRA makes a backdoor Roth IRA messy, and that conversion has to be done exactly right. The IRS overview covers the basics.

Do I have to keep every receipt?+

Not if everything runs through a dedicated business account. The bank statement is the record. Keep receipts for large purchases that could look personal, such as a laptop, and follow the IRS recordkeeping guidance.

When does an S-corp make sense for a consultant?+

Once self-employment is your main income and there's enough profit that the payroll tax saved is well above the added cost. We usually decide with real numbers in hand, often at the December projection.
